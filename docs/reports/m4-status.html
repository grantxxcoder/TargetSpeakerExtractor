What the project is, without any terminology.

When two people talk at once, a voice assistant such as Gemini Live hears both and often reports the wrong words. This project builds a filter that sits in front of it and tries to isolate one chosen speaker — identified by a short sample of their voice recorded earlier — before the assistant listens.

Two listeners are used to judge the filter's output, and the difference between them is the point. Gemini (gemini-3.7-flash, given audio and asked what it heard) stands in for the live assistant the filter is built to serve. Whisper (faster-whisper small.en) is a conventional speech-to-text transcriber, the cheap stand-in the field would normally reach for. Both are held out of training entirely.

The contribution is not the filter. It is a way of measuring whether the filter actually helps Gemini understand the right person. That sounds obvious, but the field measures something else: how clean the audio looks on a signal analyser, or how pleasant it sounds to a person. Neither of those is the same question, and this project set out to show they can point in different directions.

The one-sentence contribution

A defined, hard-to-cheat way to measure how much of what a chosen speaker said a live voice assistant actually recovers — plus the software that computes it, and the evidence that conventional audio measures do not predict it.

The central result

We doubled the training data. Every audio measure improved and Gemini understood no more.

The same filter was trained twice, once on roughly 5,000 examples and once on roughly 10,000, with nothing else changed. Conventional audio measures all improved, as expected. What the two listeners actually recovered did not.

Why this is the thesis, and not a disappointment

Had the audio measures and the comprehension measures moved together, the project would have had no reason to exist — the field could keep using the cheap measure. They did not move together, on our own model, on three instruments that share no code. That is the argument the thesis makes, and it is now measured rather than asserted.

A published filter downloaded off the shelf beats ours substantially on our own data. The report says so plainly, and the honest reading is that our filter is limited by the amount of training data we could generate and afford to train on, not by the idea. The published filter is not merely slow. We tested it and found it needs the whole recording before it can produce anything, so it cannot be used in a live conversation at any speed. Ours can. That is the difference between a research result and something that could sit in front of a real assistant, and it is the reason the comparison is reported rather than hidden.

A prediction we got wrong

Gemini and Whisper fail in opposite directions on identical audio.

We expected Whisper to cope better with the filter's artefacts than Gemini would. The opposite happened. On our filter's output Gemini dropped fewer words than it did from the raw mixture, while Whisper dropped more. Same audio file, opposite direction.

This is recorded in the write-up as a failed prediction, because it matters: it means a cheap Whisper cannot stand in for Gemini when measuring this task, which is the assumption the whole project rests on.

Stated plainly

What has not been done, so it is not mistaken for progress.

The close comparison Priority

Our two comparison systems are far apart. That shows the measurement can separate very different systems, which is a low bar. A third filter, a small variant of our own, is designed and specified but not yet built — it would test whether the measurement separates systems that are genuinely close. For a thesis whose contribution is a measurement, that is the question that matters most.

Statistical confidence Cheap

Gemini gives slightly different answers each time it is asked. Our two filters differ by about one point, which may be smaller than that variation. The repeat measurements already exist and cost nothing further to analyse — until they are, we cannot claim the two filters differ at all.

Real recordings Planned

Everything so far is measured on audio we built ourselves, which is what makes exact measurement possible. A check on genuine meeting recordings is planned, to test whether the result carries over. Only the audio-quality measure can run there, since real recordings have no exact transcript.

Three chapters In hand

Introduction, literature review and conclusion are still to write. The results tables are complete and every number in them is measured, so these are a writing job rather than a research one.

In order, with a decision point.

  1. Build and train the second filter

    The only remaining item with a hard compute dependency. If it is not training by the end of September it will be cut, and the comparison will rest on the off-the-shelf filter already measured. That decision is recorded in advance so it is not made under pressure.

  2. Analyse the repeat measurements

    Free, and it settles whether our two filters can be said to differ at all.

  3. Write the results chapter

    Tables are complete; the argument around them is not yet written. This is the part of the thesis nobody else could write.

  4. Introduction and conclusion, then the real-recording check

    Both wait on the results being settled. The real-recording check runs if the second filter goes smoothly.

One thing I would value your view on

The second filter is expected to improve comprehension by only a small margin — the best possible version of it is worth about two points. Its value is almost entirely as a test of the measurement rather than as a better system. Is that a worthwhile use of the remaining experiment time, or is it better spent on the real-recording check and the write-up?
