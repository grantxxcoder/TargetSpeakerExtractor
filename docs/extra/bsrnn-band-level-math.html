Split band-level modeling in BSRNN

What BLSTM means, the recurrence it unrolls, and why the high-frequency half of the spectrum gets a different input.

Bidirectional · low subbandsForward only · high subbands

01The name first

BLSTM = bi-directional LSTM. Nothing bilinear appears anywhere in this paper.

02The LSTM cell

An LSTM walks along a sequence one step at a time, carrying a hidden state \(\mathbf{h}\) and a cell state \(\mathbf{c}\). At step \(k\) with input \(\mathbf{x}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{N}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{f}_k &= \sigma\!\left(\mathbf{W}_f\mathbf{x}_k + \mathbf{U}_f\mathbf{h}_{k-1} + \mathbf{b}_f\right) && \text{forget gate}\\[2pt] \mathbf{i}_k &= \sigma\!\left(\mathbf{W}_i\mathbf{x}_k + \mathbf{U}_i\mathbf{h}_{k-1} + \mathbf{b}_i\right) && \text{input gate}\\[2pt] \mathbf{o}_k &= \sigma\!\left(\mathbf{W}_o\mathbf{x}_k + \mathbf{U}_o\mathbf{h}_{k-1} + \mathbf{b}_o\right) && \text{output gate}\\[2pt] \mathbf{g}_k &= \tanh\!\left(\mathbf{W}_g\mathbf{x}_k + \mathbf{U}_g\mathbf{h}_{k-1} + \mathbf{b}_g\right) && \text{candidate}\\[6pt] \mathbf{c}_k &= \mathbf{f}_k \odot \mathbf{c}_{k-1} \;+\; \mathbf{i}_k \odot \mathbf{g}_k && \text{cell update}\\[2pt] \mathbf{h}_k &= \mathbf{o}_k \odot \tanh(\mathbf{c}_k) && \text{read-out} \end{aligned} \]

\(\sigma\) is the logistic sigmoid, \(\odot\) is elementwise product. Weights \(\mathbf{W}_\ast \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times N}\), \(\mathbf{U}_\ast \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times H}\), states in \(\mathbb{R}^{H}\).

The three gates live in \((0,1)\) and act as soft switches: how much of the old memory to erase, how much of the new candidate to write, how much of the memory to expose. The reason \(\mathbf{c}\) is the long-term store is visible in its update — it is only ever scaled and added to, never passed through a squashing nonlinearity, so \(\partial \mathbf{c}_k / \partial \mathbf{c}_{k-1} = \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{f}_k)\) and gradients survive many steps when \(\mathbf{f}_k \approx \mathbf{1}\).

03Making it bi-directional

Run two independent copies of that recurrence over the same sequence, in opposite orders, and stack their outputs at each position:

\[ \overrightarrow{\mathbf{h}}_k = \mathrm{LSTM}^{\rightarrow}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_k, \overrightarrow{\mathbf{h}}_{k-1}, \overrightarrow{\mathbf{c}}_{k-1}\right), \qquad \overleftarrow{\mathbf{h}}_k = \mathrm{LSTM}^{\leftarrow}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_k, \overleftarrow{\mathbf{h}}_{k+1}, \overleftarrow{\mathbf{c}}_{k+1}\right) \] \[ \mathbf{h}_k^{\text{bi}} = \left[\,\overrightarrow{\mathbf{h}}_k \;;\; \overleftarrow{\mathbf{h}}_k\,\right] \in \mathbb{R}^{2H} \]

The two directions share no parameters and no state. Every position ends up with context from both sides, and the output width doubles to \(2H\).

04Which axis is the sequence?

This is the part that trips people up. After the band-split module you hold

\[ \mathbf{Z} \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times T \times N}, \qquad K = 33 \text{ subbands}, \quad T \text{ frames}, \quad N \text{ features} \]

and BSRNN alternates two RNNs, \(L = 6\) times:

  • Sequence-level — recurrence over \(t = 1 \dots T\), with \(K\) folded into the batch. The familiar "across time".
  • Band-level — recurrence over \(k = 1 \dots K\), with \(T\) folded into the batch. Here the sequence is the frequency axis: step 1 is the 0–200 Hz band, step 2 is 200–400 Hz, and so on up the spectrum.

Equation 1 is the band-level one. So "bi-directional" here means low\(\rightarrow\)high frequency and high\(\rightarrow\)low frequency. It has nothing to do with temporal causality — a causal, streaming BSRNN still uses a bi-directional band RNN, because reading higher frequencies to help estimate lower ones costs zero latency.

05Equation 1, unpacked

Split the frequency axis in two: \(\hat{\mathbf{Z}}_1\) holds the subbands below 8 kHz, \(\hat{\mathbf{Z}}_2\) the subbands above. Write \(K_1 + K_2 = K\) for the two band counts. Then

\[ \mathbf{Q}_1,\;(\mathbf{h}, \mathbf{c}) \;=\; \mathrm{BLSTM}\!\left(\hat{\mathbf{Z}}_1\right), \qquad \hat{\mathbf{Z}}_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{K_1 \times T \times N}, \quad \mathbf{Q}_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{K_1 \times T \times N} \] \[ \mathbf{h} \;=\; \overrightarrow{\mathbf{h}}_{K_1}, \qquad \mathbf{c} \;=\; \overrightarrow{\mathbf{c}}_{K_1} \]

The returned states are the forward direction's final states — the ones sitting at the highest low-frequency band. Not the backward direction's.

\[ \mathbf{Q}_2 \;=\; \mathrm{LSTM}\!\left(\hat{\mathbf{Z}}_2,\; (\mathbf{h}, \mathbf{c})\right), \qquad \overrightarrow{\mathbf{h}}_{0}^{\,(2)} := \mathbf{h}, \quad \overrightarrow{\mathbf{c}}_{0}^{\,(2)} := \mathbf{c} \]

A uni-directional LSTM over the high bands, initialised from those states instead of \(\mathbf{0}\).

So \(\mathbf{Q}_2\) is not a different kind of object from \(\mathbf{Q}_1\). It is the continuation of the same walk up the spectrum, minus the return trip. The two halves are then re-joined along the band axis (the CAT in Figure 1B):

\[ \mathbf{Q} = \left[\,\mathbf{Q}_1 \;;\; \mathbf{Q}_2\,\right] \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times T \times N} \]

Why the asymmetry

Device frequency responses are unpredictable above roughly 8 kHz, and some recordings simply have no content up there — the paper's Figure 1A spectrogram is dead above 16 kHz. A fully bi-directional band RNN would carry whatever garbage sits in those bands downward into the representations used to estimate the low bands, where nearly all the perceptually important energy lives. Table 1 is the evidence: full-band bi-directional BSRNN scores 3.32 PESQ-WB against 3.45 for the 16 kHz model (rows 6 vs 7); the split version recovers most of the loss at 3.42 (row 8).

The design is therefore deliberately one-way. Clean low-frequency context is allowed to inform the high bands; the reverse path is cut. Passing \((\mathbf{h}, \mathbf{c})\) rather than zeros is what preserves that one surviving direction — without it, the two halves of the spectrum would be modelled independently and you would lose spectral continuity across the split.

The silent bug nn.LSTM(bidirectional=True) orders h_n as [layer0_fwd, layer0_bwd, layer1_fwd, …]. Taking h_n[-1] gives you the backward direction's state at band 1 — the exact opposite of what equation 1 asks for. It typechecks and trains. It just quietly discards the design.

Two further points. Both LSTMs must share the same hidden size \(H\) for the handoff to line up at all, but their outputs differ in width, so you need two output projections, not one. And this whole structure repeats at every one of the six band-and-sequence layers, each with its own pair of RNNs and its own handoff — it is not a one-time operation at the input.

Where exactly to split

The prose says 8 kHz; Figure 1 labels the halves \(K_{\le 20}\) and \(K_{>20}\). Under the paper's band scheme — twenty 200 Hz bands (to 4 kHz), six 500 Hz bands (to 7 kHz), seven 2 kHz bands (to 21 kHz) — index 20 is 4 kHz, and 8 kHz falls inside a 2 kHz band. Neither reading is fully consistent. Make the split index a config parameter, pick the band boundary nearest your intended cutoff, and record the choice. It will not be what decides whether your reimplementation works.
