\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
Voice assistants engage with users through direct, live speech-to-speech models such as Gemini Live \cite{google2026geminilive}. With the appearance of more voice-based applications, the ability to engage with these tools in acoustically challenging environments is becoming increasingly important. A particular problem is the presence of interfering speech, which a speech model misinterprets as the intended target user speech, and thus responds to words never said by the target. Secondly, live speech models are exposed to noisy environments that include reverberation, background audio and other acoustic challenges. Although live models are fairly robust to background noise on its own \cite{manakul2026audiojudge} (\autoref{sec:llms}), processing audio to remove interfering speech can introduce artefacts that the live model is not robust to \cite{iwamoto2022bad,chondhekar2025denoising}. The work in this report addresses these issues through the development of a target speaker extractor \cite{zmolikova2023overview} that aims to preprocess the audio input that is given to the live model. The extractor is constructed under the following constraints: it must be causal, process audio in real time and operate on a single channel of audio. 

In this report the \emph{listener} is the speech model that transcribes the extractor's output. The deployment target is Gemini Live \cite{google2026geminilive} though every measurement uses Gemini 3.7 Flash \cite{google2026geminiflash} as its stand-in and is used to select checkpointed models. This work asks three questions:
  \begin{description}
    \item[RQ1] Do the measures conventionally used to evaluate target speaker extraction (signal-to-distortion ratios, predicted opinion scores and an offline recogniser's word error rate) indicate how much of the target's speech the listener recovers from the extractor's output? (\autoref{sec:results_rq1})
    \item[RQ2] When an extractor suppresses the interfering speaker, what are the listener's remaining errors and how are the errors divided between words leaked from the interfering speaker and words that neither speaker said? Does reducing one type of error increase the other? (\autoref{sec:results_rq2})
    \item[RQ3] Within a causal extractor that runs in real time, does giving the network more explicit information about the target's voice reduce the listener's errors and does it improve the model's ability to interpret the target? (\autoref{sec:results_rq3})
  \end{description}

The main contribution lies not in a new architecture but in how the extractor is evaluated. An evaluation suite in which the listener's errors are categorised into leaked versus invented words is developed. To answer the three research questions, this work builds a constructed dataset of \num{9955} training trials from \num{1172} LibriSpeech \cite{panayotov2015librispeech} speakers with exact transcriptions and target-absent trials. A trial consists of at most two speakers, noise and reverberation. A held-out test set of \num{248} trials from \num{20} speakers is used for final evaluation. Secondly, a causal implementation of the REAL-TSE model \cite{wang2026realtse} with a proxy objective adapted by CARTSE \cite{li2026cartse} is developed with different methods to enhance speaker identity. Finally to test on real-world data, a set of AMI meeting recordings \cite{carletta2005ami} serves as a second test set.

The remainder of this report is structured as follows: \autoref{sec:litreview} reviews the background information and context for the target speaker extraction domain. \autoref{sec:methodology} describes the process of generating the data, the architecture of the extractor and the metrics. \autoref{sec:experiments} sets out how the model is trained, selected and evaluated. \autoref{sec:results} presents the results of the evaluation and \autoref{sec:conclusion} concludes the report with a summary and potential future work.
