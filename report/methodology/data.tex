\subsection{Data construction}
\label{sec:data}

\subsubsection{Why the data is constructed}
\label{sec:construct}
There are four main considerations for the dataset that need to be met: \textit{(i)} training requires a clean target signal as a reference for what the extractor should output, \textit{(ii)} the data must possess exact transcriptions of the target and interferer signals to allow for accurate transcription-based evaluation, \textit{(iii)} trials need to include examples where the target is absent, and \textit{(iv)} every trial has its unprocessed mixture and clean version to give the worst and best case for each trial. These considerations limit the available choices of publicly available datasets. The data thus serves all the research questions: a clean target makes the signal measures computable \textbf{(RQ1)}, exact transcriptions of both speakers make the leaked and invented word measures computable \textbf{(RQ2)}, and same-gender and low Signal-to-Interference Ratio (SIR) trials test whether the extractor picks the right voice \textbf{(RQ3)}.

Constructing the data is an established practice. The 2026 REAL-TSE challenge \cite{wang2026realtse} supplied no training set at all, and required that participants document whichever open-source data they used. After reviewing the submissions, the organisers describe the data recipe that the entrants converged on: synthesise mixtures from LibriSpeech, WHAM! noise and simulated room impulse responses, whilst varying ratios such as the Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR), SIR, reverberation time, chunk length and enrollment quality and injecting target-absent segments. The dataset constructed here follows that recipe. The organisers further observed that several of the strongest entries were built on backbones near-identical to the official baseline architecture, the BSRNN, but attributed the success to data simulation, real-data adaptation, pseudo-label filtering, loss design and post-processing rather than to architecture alone.    

\subsubsection{Trial construction}
\label{sec:trial_construction}
The audio data was collected from a variety of sources to produce a diverse dataset. Speech was chosen for target and interferer from the LibriSpeech \cite{panayotov2015librispeech} dataset, which is a corpus of read English speech derived from audiobooks. The background noise was chosen from WHAM! \cite{wichern2019wham}, which is a dataset of real-world noise recordings considering environments such as offices, streets, and cafés. The trials aim to reflect real-world extraction scenarios. The primary focus is towards a base case of a target speaker close to a recording device with an interfering speaker in a similar proximity and faint background noise. The difficulty came in constructing the dataset to be realistic enough to simulate a real-world scenario, but also not so difficult that a simple extractor of limited capacity would be unable to learn the task. A visual representation of the trial construction can be found in \autoref{fig:trial_construction}.

A single trial consists of the following components: \textit{(i)} target speech, \textit{(ii)} interferer speech, \textit{(iii)} noise bed, \textit{(iv)} the mixture of the first three components, and \textit{(v)} an enrollment clip of the target speaker. Each trial falls into one of four cases: \textit{Case 1}, both speakers present, \textit{Case 2}, the target alone, \textit{Case 3}, the other speaker alone, and \textit{Case 4}, noise only. The mixture is constructed to be 15--20 seconds long. The trials are drawn from two separate regimes: a base case and a difficult case. Base trials (60\,\%) use a higher SNR, a shorter $T_{60}$ and more target speech, and difficult trials (40\,\%) use the full range of every parameter. The SIR range is the same in both. Half of the two-speaker trials pair speakers of the same gender. To simulate room acoustics, including reverberation, room dimensions, source and microphone positions, the \texttt{pyroomacoustics} \cite{scheibler2018pyroomacoustics} package is used to simulate the room impulse response. The trials are constructed to be as realistic as possible, but also to be reproducible and controllable. Finally the noise clips that are too short are looped.
 

\paragraph{Enrollment.} 
The enrollment clip is a clean \SI{5}{\second} audio segment of the target speaker from LibriSpeech and is used to provide a reference of what the target speaker sounds like. The enrollment clip is first attempted to be drawn from a different book reading, otherwise the clip is drawn from a different chapter of the same book, otherwise the clip is drawn from a different utterance of the same chapter. The reason for this is to avoid the extractor memorising the target speech by learning to recognise the content rather than the \emph{manner} that the target speaker speaks. Again to avoid bias, the enrollment clips are simulated to come from different recording devices. This is done by applying equalisation (EQ) augmentation to the enrollment clips by applying a random EQ filter to the clip with a probability of \num{0.5}. This technique was adopted from the CARTSE \cite{li2026cartse} submission for the 2026 REAL-TSE challenge \cite{wang2026realtse}.


\paragraph{Parameter distribution choice.}
SIR is chosen uniformly from the range of $-10$ to 10 dB, and the SNR is chosen uniformly from the range of 0 to 20 dB. These two ratios simulate the relative loudness of the target speaker to the interferer and background noise, respectively. A negative number for the SIR indicates that the interferer is louder than the target speaker, and the lower the SNR, the louder the background noise is relative to the target speaker. The SIR was chosen to be symmetric around \SI{0}{\decibel} after experimentation found that if the target was always (or the majority of the time) louder than the interferer, then the extractor would just learn to follow the speech that was loudest and hence ignore the conditioning of the enrollment audio. 

Reverberation time, $T_{60}$, is drawn uniformly from \SIrange{0.25}{0.6}{\second}, which covers a carpeted office to a large room with hard surfaces. The wall absorption is solved using the Sabine relation \cite{sabine1922} rather than sampled and a room that cannot simulate the drawn reverberation time is redrawn. The remaining parameters are listed in \autoref{app:params}.

\paragraph{Trial cases.} The extractor needs example trials to learn to rely on the conditioning of the enrollment audio to identify target speech. The target-absent trials support this idea by having the model learn to stay silent when the enrollment speaker is not present. The split is as follows for these trials: 49.5\% both speakers present, 25.5\% target only, 19.6\% interferer only and 5.3\% noise only.

\paragraph{Splits.} The dataset is split into training, validation and test sets. Each of these sets has its own distinct set of speakers such that there is no overlap of speakers between the sets. The training set consists of \num{9955} trials drawn from \num{1172} unique speakers. The validation set holds 200 trials from 40 other speakers: 103 with both speakers present, 47 with the target alone, 42 with the other speaker alone and 8 with noise only. It is used during training to score every epoch and to choose the checkpoint (\autoref{sec:experiments}). A test set is kept apart as the final evaluation of the simulated data seen in \autoref{sec:results}. It holds 499 trials from 20 further speakers: 248 with both speakers present, 115 with the target alone, 109 with the other speaker alone and 27 with noise only. No model was trained, tuned or selected on it, and each model is scored on it only after its checkpoint has been chosen.

\subsubsection{Real-recording evaluation set} 
The constructed dataset is limited to simulated rooms and read speech. To provide a final evaluation of the extractor, the AMI corpus \cite{carletta2005ami} is used. The AMI corpus contains 100 hours of real recordings of meetings with multiple speakers. Trials are kept to 10--20 seconds and are cut following the method of REAL-T \cite{li2025realt}. The extractor is required to listen to a single table microphone and the enrollment audio is of the target speaker speaking alone into their headset from a different meeting. 

A difficulty is that the AMI corpus does not have clean reference signals or verbatim transcriptions. Another difficulty is that the enrollment is from a headset which can pick up other speakers and background noise, so the enrollment becomes an approximation rather than a clean reference. Finally, the mixture is far quieter than in the constructed dataset: the median level of the target is \SI{-48.6}{\decibel} on AMI, compared with \SI{-29.2}{\decibel} on the validation set. The AMI evaluation contains 300 trials from 58 meetings, totalling 77.9 minutes. There are 60 speakers and all trials are Case 1 (both speakers present). The full summary can be found in \autoref{app:ami}.
