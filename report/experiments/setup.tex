  \subsection{Training setup}
  \label{sec:training}
  The training setup is described below and considers how the training data is augmented, the optimisation and hardware used, how the final model is selected, the systems compared, and the actual training observed.
  \subsubsection{Data augmentation}
  \label{exp:augmentation}
  The training data is augmented with varying strategies to prevent overfitting. The first strategy is to provide every trial twice per epoch where the target and interferer roles are swapped. This means the same mixture requires two different outputs where the only varying information is the enrolment clip. If a clip is only used once for a single speaker, the answer remains the same which can be memorised. Therefore the swap ensures that if the model memorises one voice, it will be penalised if it fails to generalise to the other voice. The second strategy is to rotate enrolment clips for a target speaker. Each speaker per trial rotates between 3 enrolment clips so that the model does not memorise a single enrolment to a speaker. The third strategy is to randomly perturb the noise and reverberation for each trial per epoch. Finally, in the spirit of stochastic methods, a single random \SI{4}{\second} crop is taken from a random position in the trial for each epoch.

  \subsubsection{Optimisation and hardware}
  \label{exp:hyperparam}
  % AdamW, learning rate, weight decay, gradient clip, batch 3, mixed precision, LR halving, early stopping, silence warm-up, seed 42, Tesla T4
  During training the AdamW optimiser \cite{loshchilov2019adamw} was used with a base learning rate of \num{0.0005}, weight decay of \num{0.0001} and gradient clipping at \num{5.0}. A batch size of 3 was used, thus 6 samples were processed per iteration (see \autoref{exp:augmentation}) with mixed precision training. The learning rate schedule included halving the learning rate when the Whisper WER stops improving. The target-absent term in the loss, $L_{\text{abs}}$, is off for the first 4 epochs, as the model in the early epochs would just output silence as this term is easy to optimise. The model is trained on Kaggle's Tesla T4 GPU for 12 hours per session and the random seed is set to 42 for reproducibility (\ref{ai:code-hard}). 
 

  \subsubsection{Model selection}
    An early stopping mechanism after 10 epochs without improvement was available as a backstop but never triggered. The baseline ran for one session of 14 epochs. The extension ran for 14 epochs, and because its in-training word error rate was still falling at the end of that session (its best epoch was the last), training was resumed for a second session, to 28 epochs.

  The final model to base the evaluation on cannot simply be chosen by the model with the lowest validation loss as a result of the proxy training objective. The loss (\autoref{sec:objective}) is a proxy for the downstream task, where the approach in the proxy is to reward better separation. However, better separation does not guarantee better recognition (\autoref{sec:evaluation}): the epoch with the lowest loss for the extension has the highest LCF-WER of the chosen checkpoints as seen in \autoref{fig:training_curves}. Secondly, the model cannot be optimised directly against the LCF-WER as it is not differentiable. Finally a consideration is that Gemini is also too slow to run after every epoch. The model is thus chosen as follows: Whisper (\autoref{sec:offline_asr}) during training will create a shortlist of best candidate checkpoints based on the WER of the validation set. The candidates are then scored by the live judge (\autoref{sec:live_judge}) on the validation set, where the lowest WER is chosen as the final model with ties broken by fewer invented words. 

  \subsubsection{Training curves}
  \label{exp:curves}
  Both models were trained with the same loss (except the additional presence gate in the extension) and the same settings, but differ in how long they were trained. The baseline was trained for 14 epochs and the extension for 28. \autoref{fig:training_curves} shows how the validation scores of each model changed during training. The top row of \autoref{fig:training_curves} shows the breakdown of the loss for the target-present branch. The three terms of the loss, shown separately, highlight how the terms interact with each other. A typical trend seen for both baseline and the extension is that as the separation term $L_{\text{pres}}$ for training decreases (rather sharply), the validation separation very gradually decreases, and then very gradually increases again. A positive note to be seen is how the multi-resolution spectrum term, $L_{\text{MR}}$, consistently decreases for both training and validation, showing how the extractor is able to reconstruct the audio with fewer artefacts. The gain term for ensuring that the model produces audio at a similar level to the target, $L_{\text{gain}}$, follows the same trend as the separation term, which is slight decrease followed by a slight increase. Finally, the baseline model is only trained for one session, as by the validation live judge results it can be seen that on the final epoch the validation WER has returned to its epoch four score. Epoch 11 is chosen for the baseline as it has the lowest validation WER.

  Although separation is not the hallmark indicator of a good extractor for the context of the downstream task, when the term is decreasing in conjunction with the spectrum term, it is a good indication to continue training. The extension is thus trained for a second session as by the end of the first session, \textit{(i)} the Whisper WER is generally decreasing although sporadic, and \textit{(ii)} the separation term with the spectrum term is decreasing. Epochs 21 and 27 tie on validation LCF-WER (39.59\,\% against 39.23\,\%), but epoch 27 leaks fewer of the other speaker's words (20.16\,\% against 23.08\,\%) and invents more (2.56 against 2.20 words per trial). Epoch 21 is chosen because it invents fewer words. Finally, when considering the presence gate, it can be seen that the loss for the gate prior decreases steadily for both training and validation with an increase after epoch 25. This shows that the model decision for the gate to turn off or on matches the prior's speech and silence durations. However, a concerning note is in the loss for the gate labels. The label loss decreases from 15 to 4 in training while validation stays flat between 12.5 and 18.5, so the gate memorised when the training speakers talked. The gate is what allows speech to be passed through to the downstream model, and with the oscillation in the validation loss, it is clear that the model had not learnt anything in the training set that generalises to the validation set. Therefore, the gate is not used in the final model and the extension is evaluated without it.

  \subsubsection{Systems compared}
  \label{exp:systems}
  % Define the five once: No processing, Baseline, Extension, WeSep, Target alone (perfect).
    This work evaluates three models against No processing and Target alone (\autoref{sec:anchors}): baseline BSRNN (implemented following REAL-TSE \cite{wang2026realtse}; \autoref{sec:architecture}), the extension BSRNN (\autoref{sec:e_architecture}), and WeSep \cite{wang2024wesep}. There are four main reasons to include WeSep for comparison: \textit{(i)} the REAL-TSE challenge \cite{wang2026realtse}, which this work uses as a guideline, builds its baseline system with WeSep; \textit{(ii)} the WeSep model has the same BSRNN backbone with TF-Map cue of \cite{zhang2025multi} as this work's baseline and extension, where the main architectural difference is WeSep's use of contextual embeddings rather than the single embedding per enrolment clip used by the extension, as discussed in \autoref{sec:speaker_context_embedding}; \textit{(iii)} WeSep was trained on clean Libri2Mix \cite{cosentino2020librimix} and has not heard any of the speakers in the evaluation sets, so it shows what an established model, trained on other data, recovers from these mixtures; and \textit{(iv)} WeSep provides a stronger offline reference for extracting the target. The WeSep model itself is non-causal and almost four times the size of the baseline (27.2\,M versus 7.19\,M parameters), and thus shows what extraction can achieve when latency and model size are not a concern. WeSep was not trained on data that contained noise or reverberation, whereas trials in this work contain both. Secondly, WeSep itself has a causal separator; however, the features are normalised over the entire clip before the separator. This setup means that every output frame depends on audio that has not arrived yet and thus the model cannot be streamed and is treated as an offline system. Finally, the results of WeSep are not comparable to the results found in the REAL-TSE challenge \cite{wang2026realtse} due to varying data, metrics and protocols. 

 


 \subsection{Evaluation setup}
 The evaluation setup is described below and considers the listeners used, the latency measures, and the evaluation metrics. The evaluation metrics are described in \autoref{sec:metrics} and the results of the evaluation are presented in \autoref{sec:results}.
  \subsubsection{Listeners}
  \label{sec:transcription}

  \paragraph{Offline ASR model.}
  \label{sec:offline_asr}
  The offline transcriber is the \texttt{faster-whisper} implementation \cite{faster_whisper} of the Whisper \cite{radford2023whisper} model, run with the \texttt{large-v3-turbo} checkpoint. This is Whisper large-v3 with its decoder pruned from 32 layers to 4 and then fine-tuned, giving 809\,M parameters against the 1{,}550\,M of large-v3 \cite{whisper_turbo}. The large Whisper variants are a common choice for scoring how intelligible generated and extracted speech is \cite{anastassiou2024seedtts,wang2025solospeech}. Decoding was greedy, with a single sampling temperature of zero and no conditioning on previously decoded text, so the same audio always produces the same transcript, and the language was fixed to English. The smaller \texttt{small.en} checkpoint (244\,M parameters) is used only inside the training loop, where it transcribes a validation sample every epoch to shortlist checkpoints and schedule the learning rate. 


  \paragraph{Live judge.}
  \label{sec:live_judge}
  The live listener is \texttt{gemini-3.7-flash} \cite{google2026geminiflash}, which was connected to via the \texttt{google-genai} Python package through the Application Programming Interface (API). The judge, like the ASR model, does not separate anything. The Gemini model is prompted to transcribe what it can hear in the extractor's output. The live model is the tool for which this work is being built and so its result is the most important with respect to the overall evaluation of the extractor. The prompt used can be found in \autoref{app:prompt} and is deliberately worded to avoid instructing the judge to perform extraction. The judge is also prompted to provide a structured response with a status field alongside the transcript. The status field is used to indicate whether the judge heard speech or not, and if it did not hear speech, the transcript is left empty. This allows for a clear distinction for whether the model hallucinates as the status field can be used to determine whether the model heard speech.

  \paragraph{Why Gemini Flash and not Gemini Live.}
  \label{sec:flash}
  Gemini Live, although the target deployment of this work, is not used in the development of the extractor. The main research concern is not \emph{when} the model replies, but \emph{what} the model understood from the extraction. Gemini Live receives audio as a stream, decides from pauses whether a target has finished speaking and can interrupt. Since it ends the input when it detects silence, a trial will cut short when the target pauses, or when an interferer is masked. Gemini Live will reply then to a fragment of the trial, and the score would reflect where Live decided the turn ended rather than how well the extractor performed. Gemini 3.7 Flash however can receive a whole trial and score against a full transcript. The extractor itself does not depend on which model listens, thus Live only provides additional difficulty for few justifiable reasons in the development process. The only benefit in using Live would be to measure turn-taking, but this is not the focus of this work. 

  \paragraph{Speech gating.}
  \label{sec:speech_gate}
  The Silero VAD v6.2.1 \cite{silero2021vad} speech gate is implemented to filter out any audio that appears to contain no speech and acts as a pre-filter for the transcription tools. A clip without detectable speech is scored as an empty transcript without the need of calling the listener. This method is supported by \cite{whisper_hallucination_2025} where the authors show that this method reduces inventing words on silence and mirrors what a deployed system would do. The gated output also shows missed words that a target said but were suppressed, and secondly shows correct output of silence when the target is absent.
  
  \subsubsection{Latency measures}
  Latency is measured against a 200--300\,ms end-to-end budget. There are two components to consider: time to consume and time to process the audio. The real-time factor (RTF) is the ratio of the time taken to process the audio to the length of the audio, and for a model to be able to keep up with real-time audio, the RTF must be less than 1. The second component considers the latency created by the architecture itself which cannot be improved by faster hardware. To produce the output frame for a given window, the model must first have received that entire window. This forces a delay of the processing of the window as per \autoref{sec:stft}.
