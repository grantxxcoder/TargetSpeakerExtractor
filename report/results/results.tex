\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}


\subsection{Why the metric is needed}
\label{sec:results_metric}
The distinction between \autoref{sec:icr} and \autoref{sec:fr} matters when investigating the insertion and substitution error of the LCF-WER metric. With Gemini as the listener, the fabrication and leakage error trade against each other. Two fine tunes were run on the extension where 6 additional epochs were trained. As shown in \autoref{fig:tradeoff}, the interferer weighted fine tune shows the clear reduction in how much the interferer speaker's content is leaked into the output, but at the expense of more invented words (as more artefacts are introduced hence more hallucination). This is shown by the mean percentage of the interferer words leaked into the output improving by 3.26 percentage points, but the number of invented words increasing. The artefact weighted fine tune shows the opposite situation with a heavy increase in leaked words but slight reduction in invented words. Each error has a different cause and solution, and thus should be treated separately, which would be hidden by the LCF-WER metric alone.

\subsection{Content fidelity}
\label{sec:results_content}
 The content fidelity metrics are used to measure how the live artificial intelligence system is able to interpret the audio after the extraction process. The live system is a black box, non-deterministic system, the stability of the interpretation of the audio is also important to consider. The sections to follow consider the content fidelity for a live judge on both real and the simulated data.

 The baseline improves the LCF-WER by 9.68 points, which is a 15.4\,\% relative improvement over the floor. There is a decisive improvement however in the baseline model's performance over the floor with regard to including an interfering speaker's content, as the average percentage of interfering words included in the output is 17.15 points lower than the floor. This came at a trade-off with the number of hallucinated words. The baseline system subsequently has fabricated/invented more words on average per trial as seen in the table. Finally, the baseline system also more frequently fabricates, as indicated by FR@2. Therefore, both the frequency and quantity of fabrication have increased over passing the audio unprocessed. 

The baseline system is a clear improvement over the floor, which is indicative that the extractor has begun to find a signal in the mixture that is associated with the target speaker. For the extension, the inclusion of the pretrained ECAPA speaker encoder and passing the enrollment audio in parts instead of a single embedding has markedly improved the performance of the extractor. The LCF-WER has improved by 13.67 points over the baseline, which is a 25.7\,\% relative improvement. The extension has both reduced the share of leaked words per trial and the frequency of leakage by substantial amounts. Unlike the leakage, the extension did not drastically invent less words per trial, with only a slight improvement from 2.52 to 2.20 words per trial. However, both baseline and the extension invent well above the floor which is at 1.27 words invented per trial.

\autoref{fig:judge_runs} demonstrates the stability of the LCF-WER across the three independent runs measured per clip. The baseline demonstrates the worst stability, changing by 1.82 points, with greater variability as compared to floor. The extension has the best stability even when compared to WeSep. On single clips, there are outlier performance changes of up to 209 points (when one run results in 209 more errors per 100 reference words than another run), these outliers could be incredibly difficult cases that are not indicative of the overall performance of the system. However, as seen in the bar plot, the extension on average has the smallest changes in overall performance across the three runs, although performs much worse on outlier cases as seen in the box plot.

Table \ref{tab:content_cases} highlights where the extension shines. For the cases where the extractor needs to either suppress only background noise surrounding a target or only interfering speech, the extension performs better than WeSep. Secondly, if the extractor needs to suppress background noise such that a voice activity detector (VAD) does not detect any speech, the extension is able to do this more often than WeSep getting 18 noisy clips suppressed as compared against WeSep's 7. The improvement in the extension's performance can be attributed to the diversity of training examples that include those specific cases. Interestingly, all the models perform worse on the target-only case than just leaving the target unprocessed. This is likely due to the robustness of Gemini to background noise, leaving the extractor model to introduce artefacts that are misinterpreted by Gemini. Overall, the extension has shown improvement over WeSep in different acoustic scenarios, although it still falls short when considering pure speech extraction with multiple speakers.

\subsection{Conventional metrics}
\label{sec:results_signal}
The three kinds of separation considered in this project are the target from the background noise (distortion), interference from other speakers and finally from artefacts introduced by the extractor system. The signal-domain separation results are shown in \autoref{tab:conventional}. These metrics highlight what the extractor system is doing in the signal domain and can best be used to understand the trade-offs between the different types of separation. A note about the values in the table: SAR for the floor and ceiling are both at +30 dB, since these systems do not contain a model there can be no artefacts introduced.

The extension has significantly improved the SIR metric over the baseline, and is comparable to the WeSep model. The gap in performance between the extension and WeSep therefore seems to lie in the SAR and SDR metrics. Therefore when considering how to improve the extension, the next iteration should potentially target mechanisms that reduce the artefacts introduced by the extractor system as artefacts are hypothesised to increase the fabrication rate. The baseline has the highest model SAR metric. This should not be attributed to the model being better at reducing artefacts, but rather due to the simplistic nature of the model design. The baseline model is not as aggressive in masking out the interfering speaker's content, and therefore does not introduce as many artefacts into the output. 

Considering P.808 in isolation, the extension has a comparable overall quality score to WeSep. By the one perceptual quality metric the extension produces audio that is as pleasing to a human listener (by the P.808 model standards) as the WeSep model. When considering the other three metrics, the WeSep model has a better SIG and OVRL score than the extension. The extension scores lower than the floor for both the SIG and OVRL metrics, and yet as seen in the content fidelity results, the extension has improved the content fidelity over the floor by a significant margin. This demonstrates that the perceptual quality metrics are not good indicators for the purposes of this research project. The metric in itself is subjective to the actual perceptual system and does not provide indication that what sounds good to a human listener is necessarily better for the content fidelity of the target speaker's audio.

The WeSep model is not able to meet the requirements and therefore cannot be used in a live system. Furthermore, the extension did not miss any chunks and the worst case delay was 196 milliseconds, which is below the 200--300 millisecond requirement.
