This section covers the three research questions with results to answer them individually. In short, the signal metrics rank the systems in the same order as the Gemini LCF-WER, but the perceptual metrics do not. Crucially, however, despite the rankings, the signal metrics do not explain why Gemini performs better or worse. Secondly, the nature of the masking architecture causes the trade-off between the invention and leakage errors. Finally, the speaker-specific information provides the extractor with meaningful knowledge to better isolate the target speaker and still fits in the causal latency budget.

The extension has improved the SIR metric over the baseline, and is comparable to the WeSep model (SIR: baseline 5.43, extension 9.17, WeSep 10.34\,dB). The gap in performance between the extension and WeSep therefore seems to lie in the SAR and SDR metrics (SAR: extension 6.28, WeSep 8.26\,dB; SDR: extension 2.29, WeSep 4.68\,dB). The baseline has the highest model SAR metric (SAR: baseline 8.58, WeSep 8.26, extension 6.28\,dB). This should not be attributed to the model being better at reducing artefacts, but rather to the simplistic nature of the model design. The baseline model is not as aggressive in masking out the interfering speaker's content (SIR: baseline 5.43, extension 9.17\,dB), and therefore does not introduce as many artefacts into the output. Considering P.808 in isolation, the extension has a comparable overall quality score to WeSep (P.808: extension 3.03, WeSep 3.01). By the one perceptual quality metric the extension produces audio that is as pleasing to a human listener as the WeSep model. When considering the other three metrics, the WeSep model has a better SIG and OVRL score than the extension. The extension scores lower than the floor for both the SIG and OVRL metrics.

 The WeSep model clearly shows the highest SDR and SIR of the models, with a clear lowest LCF-WER (25.80\,\%) as seen in Table~\ref{tab:rq1_gemini}, however, when considering each metric individually, a single signal metric does not explain the better Gemini performance. For example, the extension has comparable SIR to WeSep, the baseline beats WeSep on SAR, and it is already established in \autoref{sec:llms} that LAMs are robust against noise. Therefore by the signal metrics alone, WeSep can be established as a better separation model, but it does not explain why Gemini performs better on WeSep's output. Next, the perceptual quality metrics are not good indicators for LCF-WER either. The metric in itself is subjective to the actual perceptual system and does not provide any indication that what sounds good to a human listener is necessarily better for the content fidelity of the target speaker's audio. This is established by the extension having comparable perceptual scores to WeSep, but a much higher LCF-WER.

 The baseline improves the LCF-WER by 9.68 points, which is a 15.38\,\% relative improvement over the floor, and an improvement with regard to including less of the interfering speaker's content, as the average percentage of interfering words included in the output is 21.70 points lower than the floor (mean leak: no processing 63.90, baseline 42.20\,\%). This came at a trade-off with the number of hallucinated words. The baseline system consequently invents more words on average per trial as seen in \autoref{fig:content_judge} (invented per trial: no processing 1.27, baseline 2.52). Finally, the baseline system also more frequently fabricates, as indicated by FR@2 (no processing 30.74, baseline 49.84\,\%). Therefore, both the frequency and quantity of fabrication have increased over passing the audio unprocessed. The extension has both reduced the share of leaked words per trial and the frequency of leakage by substantial amounts. Unlike the leakage, the extension did not drastically invent fewer words per trial, with only a slight improvement from 2.52 to 2.20 words per trial. However, both the baseline and the extension invent well above the floor which is at 1.27 words invented per trial. Another important finding is that \emph{all} models invent more than passing the mixture unprocessed which indicates how the model introduces the means of fabrication through artefacts.

Four fine-tunes were run on the extension where 6 additional epochs were trained. As shown in \autoref{tab:tradeoff}, when an error is focused on, the relevant score improves over both the control and the other fine-tune. However, in each case, this comes at the expense of the other error. Crucially, it is a hypothesis that these errors are not trade-offs of each other, but a by-product of the trade-off of the masking architecture. By weighting by a different error, the masking behaviour of the extractor changes. With reference to the control, none of the fine-tunes improves the LCF-WER score on average, at most performing within the noise of the control as seen for the leak-weighted fine-tune (LCF-WER change from the control: leak weight $+0.47$). Detrimentally, any artefact-related fine-tune has a clear negative impact on the LCF-WER score with both runs showing an increase in the score (LCF-WER: control 38.24, artefact-weighted 47.58, both weights 42.87\,\%).

\paragraph{The trade-off of the masking architectures.} As a masking architecture focuses on removing interfering content, by masking frames that contain the interfering speaker's content, the overlapping frames that contain the target speaker's content are also masked. The hypothesis is that from the perspective of the extractor, a single frame only contains raw audio of unknown mixture quantities of different sources. As a result, the more aggressive the extractor becomes in removing content from a frame, the more likely it is to remove the target speaker's content as well. Therefore, more aggressive interferer suppression leads to a quicker degradation of audio quality, which then is misinterpreted by the listener as fabricated content. Conversely, the weaker the suppression of the interfering speaker, the more likely that the interferer content is included in the output. The trade-off between the two errors is hypothesised to be a result of the masking procedure and not between the errors themselves.

\paragraph{Case breakdown of how the extractor performs.} \autoref{fig:content_cases} highlights where the extension shines. For the cases where the extractor needs to either suppress only background noise surrounding a target or only interfering speech, the extension performs better than WeSep. Secondly, if the extractor needs to suppress the other speaker such that a voice activity detector (VAD) does not detect any speech, the extension is able to do this more often than WeSep getting 18 of the 42 clips silenced as compared against WeSep's 7 (Case 3 outputs silenced). The improvement in the extension's performance can be attributed to the diversity of training examples that include those specific cases. Interestingly, all the models perform worse on the target-only case than just leaving the target unprocessed (Case 2 LCF-WER: no processing 1.65, baseline 2.48, extension 3.56, WeSep 5.40\,\%). This is likely due to the robustness of Gemini to background noise, leaving the extractor model to introduce artefacts that are misinterpreted by Gemini. Overall, the extension has shown improvement over WeSep in different acoustic scenarios, although it still falls short when considering pure speech extraction with two speakers (Case 1 LCF-WER: extension 39.59, WeSep 25.80\,\%).

\subsection{RQ3: Voice information in real time}
\label{sec:results_rq3}
\label{sec:results_latency}
\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics{figures/voice_picking.pdf}
\caption{How often each extractor returns the voice it is asked for, on the two-voice validation pairs (\ref{ai:diagrams}).}
\label{fig:voice_picking}
\end{figure}
The extension system is a clear improvement over the baseline, which is indicative that the extractor has begun to find a signal in the mixture that is associated with the target speaker, but it must now be considered whether the extension can be used in real-time applications. More specifically, can the extractor better choose the right speaker in the latency budget? This is demonstrated by \autoref{fig:voice_picking}, which highlights how even in the difficult case where both speakers are the same gender, the extension is able to pick the right speaker more often than the baseline. In the same-gender case, males typically will share most of their energy in the lower frequencies, and females in the higher frequencies. Previously in the baseline, the extractor would just have to guess which speaker to extract (same gender, baseline 52.6\,\%). However, now the speaker cue change and the ECAPA embedding provide sufficient information to isolate the correct speaker. The latency and throughput results are shown in \autoref{fig:latency}. The baseline and the extension are able to meet the real-time factor and latency requirements for a live system (RTF $<$ 1.0). The WeSep model is not able to meet the requirements and therefore cannot be used in a live system (WeSep RTF 2.85, mean delay 348.3\,ms). Furthermore, the extension did not miss any chunks and the worst-case delay was 196\,ms, which is below the 200--300\,ms requirement.

\autoref{tab:ami_results} shows Gemini's results on the AMI meeting recordings of \autoref{app:ami}, and \autoref{fig:sets} compares the LCF-WER on the validation, test and AMI sets. The conclusion is the same as for the simulated data: the extension improves the LCF-WER over doing nothing, although the offline WeSep system performs better for the strict extraction task. WeSep has the lowest LCF-WER (80.56\,\%), followed by the extension (91.26\,\%) and then no processing (115.51\,\%). A demonstration of how the model distorts the speech can be seen by the target words missed metric, where Gemini is able without any processing to only miss 3.46\,\% of the target words, but even with WeSep, Gemini misses 27.71\,\% of the target words. This indicates how the extractor removes the background interfering speech at the cost of degrading the target speech. Finally, there is an increase in artefacts introduced by the extractor, as seen in the increase in invented words per trial (1.48 to 1.83) and rate of fabrication (FR@2, 38.33\,\% to 49.46\,\%) for the extension, which is an expected result from the simulated dataset.
