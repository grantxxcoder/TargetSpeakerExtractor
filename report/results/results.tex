This section covers the three research questions with results to answer them individually. In short, the signal metrics rank the systems in the same order as the Gemini LCF-WER, but the perceptual metrics do not. Crucially, however, despite the rankings, the signal metrics do not explain why Gemini performs better or worse. Secondly, cutting leaked words comes with more invented words. The hypothesis, tested in \autoref{sec:results_rq2}, is that both errors come from how hard the mask suppresses frames the two speakers share. Finally, the speaker-specific information provides the extractor with meaningful knowledge to better isolate the target speaker and still fits in the causal latency budget.

The extension has improved the SIR metric over the baseline, and is comparable to the WeSep model (SIR: baseline 5.43, extension 9.17, WeSep 10.34\,dB). The gap in performance between the extension and WeSep therefore seems to lie in the SAR and SDR metrics (SAR: extension 6.28, WeSep 8.26\,dB; SDR: extension 2.29, WeSep 4.68\,dB). The baseline has the highest model SAR metric (SAR: baseline 8.58, WeSep 8.26, extension 6.28\,dB). This should not be attributed to the model being better at reducing artefacts, but rather to the simplistic nature of the model design. The baseline model is not as aggressive in masking out the interfering speaker's content (SIR: baseline 5.43, extension 9.17\,dB), and therefore does not introduce as many artefacts into the output. Considering P.808 in isolation, the extension has a comparable overall quality score to WeSep (P.808: extension 3.03, WeSep 3.01). On P.808 alone, the extension's predicted listener rating matches WeSep's (3.03 against 3.01). When considering the other three metrics, the WeSep model has a better SIG and OVRL score than the extension. The extension scores lower than the floor for both the SIG and OVRL metrics.

 The WeSep model clearly shows the highest SDR and SIR of the models, with a clear lowest LCF-WER (34.08\,\%) as seen in Table~\ref{tab:rq1_gemini}, however, when considering each metric individually, a single signal metric does not explain the better Gemini performance. For example, the extension has comparable SIR to WeSep, the baseline beats WeSep on SAR, and it is already established in \autoref{sec:llms} that LAMs are robust against noise. Therefore by the signal metrics alone, WeSep can be established as a better separation model, but it does not explain why Gemini performs better on WeSep's output. Next, the perceptual quality metrics are not good indicators for LCF-WER either. DNSMOS predicts how pleasant the audio sounds to people, not how much of the target's speech a listener can recover. Audio that sounds good is not necessarily better for content fidelity. This is established by the extension having comparable perceptual scores to WeSep, but a much higher LCF-WER.

 The baseline lowers the LCF-WER by 3.59 points over the floor (no processing 52.05, baseline 48.46\,\%), but the 95\,\% interval of this change, [\num{-9.33}, \num[retain-explicit-plus]{+1.97}], includes zero, so on the test set the baseline cannot be distinguished from passing the mixture unprocessed. It does include less of the interfering speaker's content, as the average percentage of interfering words in the output is 14.67 points lower than the floor (mean leak: no processing 47.89, baseline 33.22\,\%). This came at a trade-off with the number of invented words. The baseline system consequently invents more words on average per trial as seen in \autoref{fig:content_judge} (invented per trial: no processing 1.48, baseline 2.24). The baseline system also more frequently fabricates, as indicated by FR@2 (no processing 30.24, baseline 41.13\,\%). Therefore, both the frequency and quantity of fabrication have increased over passing the audio unprocessed. The extension has both reduced the share of leaked words per trial (baseline 33.22, extension 21.15\,\%) and the frequency of leakage (ICR@2: baseline 47.37, extension 32.93\,\%) by substantial amounts. Unlike the leakage, the extension does not invent fewer words than the baseline (2.24 against 2.26 words per trial), and it fabricates two or more words in more trials (FR@2: baseline 41.13, extension 47.74\,\%). Both invent well above the floor of 1.48 words per trial. Another important finding is that \emph{all} models invent more than passing the mixture unprocessed (baseline 2.24, extension 2.26, WeSep 2.07), which is consistent with the extractor's artefacts being heard as words.

Four fine-tunes were run on the extension where 6 additional epochs were trained. As shown in \autoref{tab:tradeoff}, adding the leak weight reliably cut leaked words, against both the control (\num{-3.26}) and the artefact-weighted run (\num{-6.39}). Adding the artefact weight cut invented words only on top of the leak weight but against the control, the change is within the noise [\num{-0.65}, \num[retain-explicit-plus]{+0.25}]. However, in each case, this comes at the expense of the other error. Crucially, it is a hypothesis that these errors are not trade-offs of each other, but a by-product of the trade-off of the masking architecture. By weighting by a different error, the masking behaviour of the extractor changes. With reference to the control, none of the fine-tunes improves the LCF-WER score on average, at most performing within the noise of the control as seen for the leak-weighted fine-tune (LCF-WER change from the control: leak weight $+0.47$). Detrimentally, any artefact-related fine-tune has a clear negative impact on the LCF-WER score with both runs showing an increase in the score (LCF-WER: control 38.24, artefact-weighted 47.58, both weights 42.87\,\%).

\paragraph{The trade-off of the masking architectures.} As a masking architecture focuses on removing interfering content, by masking frames that contain the interfering speaker's content, the overlapping frames that contain the target speaker's content are also masked. The hypothesis is that from the perspective of the extractor, a single frame only contains raw audio of unknown mixture quantities of different sources. As a result, the more aggressive the extractor becomes in removing content from a frame, the more likely it is to remove the target speaker's content as well. Therefore, more aggressive interferer suppression leads to a quicker degradation of audio quality, which then is misinterpreted by the listener as fabricated content. Conversely, the weaker the suppression of the interfering speaker, the more likely that the interferer content is included in the output. The trade-off between the two errors is hypothesised to be a result of the masking procedure and not between the errors themselves.

\paragraph{Case breakdown of how the extractor performs.} \autoref{fig:content_cases} highlights where the extension shines. For the cases where the extractor needs to either suppress only background noise surrounding a target or only interfering speech, the extension performs better than WeSep. Secondly, if the extractor needs to suppress the other speaker such that a voice activity detector (VAD) does not detect any speech, the extension is able to do this more often than WeSep getting 18 of the 42 clips silenced as compared against WeSep's 7 (Case 3 outputs silenced). The improvement in the extension's performance can be attributed to the diversity of training examples that include those specific cases. Interestingly, all the models perform worse on the target-only case than just leaving the target unprocessed (Case 2 LCF-WER: no processing 1.65, baseline 2.48, extension 3.56, WeSep 5.40\,\%). This is likely due to the robustness of Gemini to background noise, leaving the extractor model to introduce artefacts that are misinterpreted by Gemini. Overall, the extension has shown improvement over WeSep in different acoustic scenarios, although it still falls short when considering pure speech extraction with two speakers (Case 1 LCF-WER: extension 39.59, WeSep 25.80\,\%).

\subsection{RQ3: Voice information in real time}
\label{sec:results_rq3}
\label{sec:results_latency}
\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics{figures/voice_picking.pdf}
\caption{How often each extractor returns the voice it is asked for, on the two-voice validation pairs (\ref{ai:diagrams}).}
\label{fig:voice_picking}
\end{figure}
The extension system is a clear improvement over the baseline, which is indicative that the extractor has begun to find a signal in the mixture that is associated with the target speaker, but it must now be considered whether the extension can be used in real-time applications. More specifically, can the extractor better choose the right speaker in the latency budget? This is demonstrated by \autoref{fig:voice_picking}, which highlights how even in the difficult case where both speakers are the same gender, the extension is able to pick the right speaker more often than the baseline. When the two speakers differ in gender, their voices sit in different pitch ranges, which is the easier case. When they share a gender, the voices overlap and the extractor must use finer differences. Here the baseline picked the right voice 52.63\,\% of the time and is not better than chance, and the extension 72.37\,\%, a gain of 19.74 points. The speaker cue change and the ECAPA embedding provide sufficient information to isolate the correct speaker. The latency and throughput results are shown in \autoref{fig:latency}. The baseline and the extension are able to meet the real-time factor and latency requirements for a live system (RTF $<$ 1.0). The WeSep model is not able to meet the requirements and therefore cannot be used in a live system (WeSep RTF 2.85, mean delay 348.3\,ms). Furthermore, the extension did not miss any chunks and the worst-case delay was 196\,ms, which is below the 200--300\,ms requirement.
