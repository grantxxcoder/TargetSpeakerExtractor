\subsection{Why the metric is needed}
\label{sec:results_metric}
The distinction between \autoref{sec:icr} and \autoref{sec:fr} matters when investigating the insertion and substition error of the LCF-WER metric. With Gemini as the listener, the fabrication and leakage error trade against each other. To demonstrate this, two fine tunes were run on the extension model where 5 additional epochs were trained: one run weighted the interferer speaker audio penalised the interferer audio more heavily, and the second penalised the artefact error. The fine tune was done on the validation set to demonstrate the tradeoff of the metric and not necessarily to improve the extension (in which the fine tuning aas a strategy did not show improvement). The results of the two fine tunes are shown in \autoref{tab:metric_evidence}. Both fine tunes show the LCF-WER metric worsening but their reasons are different. The interferer weighted fine tune shows the clear reduction in how much the interferer speaker's content is leaked into the output, but at the expense of more invented words (as more artefacts are introduced hence more hallucination). This is shown by the mean percentage of the interferer words leaked into the output improving by 3.26 percentage points, but the number of invented words increasing. The second fine shows the opposite situation with a heavy increase in leaked words but slight reduction in invented words.

\autoref{tab:metric_evidence} shows how changes to an extractor move what each listener recovers, split into the errors the metric separates.

\subsection{Content fidelity}
\label{sec:results_content}
The main metrics to consider for this project are the performance of our extractor system with respect to the content fidelity on a live system. The content fidelity metrics are used to measure how the live artificial intelligence system is able to interpret the audio after the extraction process. The end goal is for the extractor to isolate and process the target speaker's audio in such a way for there to be minimal loss of content fidelity. Secondly, since the live system is a black box, non-deterministic system, the stability of the interpretation of the audio is also important to consider. 
The sections to follow consider the content fidelity for both a live judge and an offline transcriber.

 The baseline improves the LCF-WER by 9.68 points, which is a 15.4\,\% relative improvement over the floor. There is a decisive improvement however in the baseline model's performance over the floor with regard to including an interfering speaker's content, as the average percentage of interfering words included in the output is 17.15 points lower than the floor. This came at a trade-off with the number of hallucinated words. The baseline system subsequently has fabricated/invented more words on average per trial as seen in the table. Finally, the baseline system also more frequently fabricates, as indicated by FR@2. Therefore, both the frequency and quantity of fabrication have increased over passing the audio unprocessed. 

Overall, the baseline system is a clear improvement over the floor, which is indicative that the extractor has begun to find a signal in the mixture that is associated with the target speaker. The encouraging results can be seen when comparing the extension against the baseline and WeSep. For the extension, the inclusion of the pretrained ECAPA speaker encoder and passing the enrollment audio in parts instead of a single embedding has markedly improved the performance of the extractor. The LCF-WER has improved by 13.67 points over the baseline, which is a 25.7\,\% relative improvement. However, the most encouraging part of the results is how the extension has both reduced the number of leaked words per trial and the frequency of leakage by substantial amounts. The improvement can be attributed to the speaker specific information being passed to the extractor through the ECAPA speaker encoder. Unlike the leakage, the extension did not drastically invent less words per trial, with only a slight improvement from 2.52 to 2.20 words per trial. However, both baseline and extension invent well above the floor (1.27).

To get a better idea of how stable the live judge is on the same audio, \autoref{fig:judge_runs} demonstrates how the LCF-WER changed across the three independent runs but measured per clip instead of the overall average. The first observation is the confirmation of the extreme variability of the live judge when given audio extracted by the baseline. In fact, the baseline has worse variability than the floor and the highest average in change of performance between the three runs. The extension has the best stability even when compared to WeSep. On single clips, there are outlier performance changes of up to 209 points (when one run results in 209 more errors per 100 reference words than another run), these outliers could be incredibly difficult cases that are not indicative of the overall performance of the system. However, as seen in the bar plot, the extension on average has the smallest changes in overall performance across the three runs. Measured from the extension's three runs, the judge alone moves a system's score by about 1.2 points from one run to the next (standard error 1.24, a 95\,\% range of $\pm 2.43$ points; 0.72 for the 3-run mean), so two systems judged once each cannot be told apart below about 4 points.

Table \ref{tab:content_cases} highlights where the extension shines. For the cases where the extractor needs to either suppress only background noise surrounding a target or only interfering speech, the extension performs better than WeSep. Secondly, if the extractor needs to suppress background noise such that a voice activity detector (VAD) does not detect any speech, the extension is able to do this more often than WeSep getting 18 noisy clips suppressed as compared against WeSep's 7. On these 42 clips the difference in words per trial is within noise ($-1.45$, 95\,\% CI $-7.05$ to $+4.10$). The improvement in the extension's performance can be attributed to the diversity of training examples that include those specific cases. Interestingly, all the models perform worse on the target-only case than just leaving the target unprocessed. This is likely due to the robustness of Gemini to background noise, leaving the extractor model to introduce artefacts that are misinterpreted by Gemini. Overall, the extension has shown improvement over WeSep in different acoustic scenarios, although it still falls short when considering pure speech extraction with multiple speakers.

\subsection{Error composition}
\label{sec:results_errors}
The LCF-WER metric is a combination of three different types of errors to form the word error rate: substitution, deletion and insertion. The metric is useful for breaking down the weaknesses of the extractor system and to better understand how the system is introducing error. For example, a system with high deletions is indicative of over suppression of the target speaker's content, a system with high insertions is indicative of hallucination or leakage, and finally a system with high substitutions is indicative of misinterpretation of the target speaker's content. The error composition for both the live judge and the offline transcriber is shown in \autoref{tab:error_split}.

The first observation is related to the live judge and how it performs when given a raw mixture. As expected, the floor system has a high insertion rate of 28.08\,\% which is indicative of the interfering speaker's content being included in the output and then a high substitution rate of 29.22\,\% which is indicative of the live judge misinterpreting the target speaker's content. The extension has significantly improved the SIR metric over the baseline, and is comparable to the WeSep model. The gap in performance between the extension and WeSep therefore seems to lie in the SAR and SDR metrics. Therefore when considering how to improve the extension, the next iteration should potentially target mechanisms that reduce the artefacts introduced by the extractor system as artefacts are hypothesised to increase the fabrication rate. The baseline has the highest model SAR metric. This should not be attributed to the model being better at reducing artefacts, but rather due to the simplistic nature of the model design. The baseline model is not as aggressive in masking out the interfering speaker's content, and therefore does not introduce as many artefacts into the output. 

Considering P.808 in isolation, the extension has a comparable overall quality score to WeSep. By the one perceptual quality metric our extension produces audio that is as pleasing to a human listener (by the P.808 model standards) as the WeSep model. When considering the other three metrics, the WeSep model has a better SIG and OVRL score than the extension. The extension scores lower than the floor for both the SIG and OVRL metrics, and yet as seen in the content fidelity results, the extension has improved the content fidelity over the floor by a significant margin. This demonstrates that the perceptual quality metrics are not good indicators for the purposes of this research project, which is to improve the content fidelity of the target speaker's audio. The metric in itself is subjective to the actual perceptual system and does not provide indication that what sounds good to a human listener is necessarily better for the content fidelity of the target speaker's audio.
